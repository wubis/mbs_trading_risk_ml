PART 2: MODELING LOSS

Modeling setup and EDA

I treated this as an estimate based on the current state of each loan. That choice follows from predictions.csv, which includes updated occupancy, updated NOI, and delinquency flags. These fields would be leakage if the goal were to make a prediction at origination. To show how much they matter, part2.py also fits a model that uses origination-time fields only. I excluded loan_id and propname from both models.

The labeled data has 39,699 loans across 36,978 properties. Severity is zero for 83.93% of the loans. There are also 108 severity values above the stated upper bound of one, including a maximum of 13.6473. The main missing fields are sqft (12,870 merged loan rows), occ_at_orig (2,027), occ (1,609), proptypelong (1,206), and year_built (70). Numeric imputation and missing-value indicators are fitted using the training data only.

A random row split could put two loans from the same property on opposite sides of the split. That affects 2,033 properties in this data. I used a fixed 80/20 GroupShuffleSplit on propname instead. It produced 31,790 training loans and 7,909 validation loans with no property overlap. The share of positive-severity loans was 16.16% in training and 15.72% in validation. A time-based test would also be useful for a production model, but there is no date showing when severity was observed. Origination date alone is not enough to build a clean forward-looking test.

I kept that holdout for the required plot and also ran three shuffled repetitions of five-fold GroupKFold. This produced 15 paired validation folds. Each property appeared in one validation fold per repetition, with no property overlap inside any fold. Preprocessing and both models were refitted from scratch for every fold.

Features used in the required current-state linear regression

1. log_loan_size: Loan balance measures exposure size. I used log1p because a small number of loans are much larger than the rest.
2. ltv_fraction: Higher leverage leaves less collateral cushion if a property must be sold.
3. occ_at_orig_fraction: Origination occupancy records the property's starting demand and underwriting condition.
4. signed_log_noi_orig: Origination NOI measures starting debt-service capacity. A signed log reduces skew without dropping negative values.
5. loan_term_years: Contractual loan term may change refinancing and maturity risk.
6. origination_year: Origination vintage can proxy for underwriting and market regimes.
7. property_age: Older properties may face more physical or functional obsolescence.
8. property_age_invalid: This flag identifies the 848 rows where the recorded building year would make the property age negative at origination. I set those ages to missing before imputation.
9. log_sqft: Property size may affect liquidity and recovery. I used log1p because square footage is strongly right-skewed.
10. occ_fraction: Current occupancy is a direct measure of the property's latest operating condition.
11. occupancy_change: The change from origination shows whether occupancy improved or deteriorated.
12. signed_log_noi_current: Current NOI measures the latest income available to support the debt. A signed log keeps negative NOI values valid.
13. noi_relative_change: Relative NOI change measures operating improvement or deterioration. I used a denominator floor and fixed clipping to control unstable ratios.
14. is_30d: Thirty-day delinquency is an observed indicator of current credit stress.
15. is_90d: Ninety-day delinquency identifies more advanced payment distress.
16. cssaproptype: The broad property type captures sector differences without using the inconsistent detailed labels.
17. msa_category: MSA category gives the model a coarse measure of market and location.
18. state: State adds more geographic detail. The encoder can handle states or labels not seen during training.
19. office_group: The Part 1 grouping lets the model distinguish office, mixed-office, and non-office exposure.

For numeric fields, I used median imputation, missing-value indicators, and standardization. For categorical fields, I normalized the text, filled missing values with the most common training value, and used one-hot encoding that ignores unseen categories. All preprocessing is fitted on the training partition. loan_id and propname remain identifiers only. I converted the raw dates into loan term and origination year. I left proptypelong out of the baseline because it has hundreds of inconsistent labels, including six labels that appear only in the scoring data.

Linear-regression results

On the property-grouped holdout, linear regression produced R² = 0.517950 and MSE = 0.018751. RMSE was 0.136936 and MAE was 0.066204. In the true-versus-predicted plot, the predictions form bands around a few average levels instead of following the 45-degree line. Predictions range from -0.1839 to 0.6805, and 31.67% are negative.

Why does ordinary linear regression perform poorly?

The target is not shaped like a standard continuous outcome. Most observations are exactly zero, while the positive observations follow a wide, right-skewed distribution. The effects of delinquency, leverage, occupancy decline, and NOI decline are also unlikely to be linear or independent of one another. Squared error gives extra weight to the long right tail, including the 108 values above one. Linear regression can also predict below zero, which is why almost one-third of its validation predictions are not economically meaningful.

Recommended alternative

I also tested a two-stage hurdle model. The first stage estimates whether a loan will have positive severity. The second estimates the size of the loss for loans with positive severity. Their product is the expected severity. Gradient-boosted trees allow nonlinear effects and interactions, and the Poisson loss used in the second stage keeps the magnitude positive. On the same holdout, the hurdle model reached R² = 0.585174, MSE = 0.016136, and MAE = 0.048309 with no negative predictions. The first stage had ROC-AUC of 0.9357, average precision of 0.8073, and a Brier score of 0.0548.

The repeated grouped results support the same choice. Mean MSE was 0.022719 for linear regression and 0.019861 for the hurdle model, a 12.58% reduction. Mean MAE fell from 0.066234 to 0.048328, a 27.03% reduction. The hurdle model had lower MSE and MAE in all 15 paired folds. Its fold-level MSE improvement ranged from 7.00% to 17.52%. The average gain is moderate, but it is consistent across property splits.

Baseline and sensitivity context

The training-mean baseline had MSE of 0.038900, and an all-zero prediction had MSE of 0.043554. The origination-only linear model had R² = 0.067295 and MSE = 0.036282. By comparison, the current-state linear model had R² = 0.517950. This large gap shows that updated operating data and delinquency status provide most of the signal. Those fields should only be used when they are available at the intended prediction date. I also capped severity at one in a separate check. That version of linear regression produced R² = 0.525254 and MSE = 0.017874, but it still generated negative predictions.

Scoring output

The hurdle model had the lowest validation MSE, so part2.py fits it again using all labeled loans and writes outputs/predictions_scored.csv. The file contains all 1,000 source rows in the original order. It has no missing or infinite predictions, and predicted severity ranges from 0.0001 to 0.7855. The script does not overwrite predictions.csv.
